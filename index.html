Heavy Spectral decomposition for Noisy Correlators in LQCD using ML

Introduction

QCD is hard to solve when low-energy states are taken into account. Given the inability to use perturbative methods in QCD in low-energy states, the most popular method to understand the interaction of quarks and gluons is through the use of lattice QCD, where QCD is embedded onto a lattice or the continuum is discretized into intervals and the resultant equations are calculated numerically.

Correlators in Lattice QCD

In lattice QCD, correlators \cite{McNeile_2004} are fundamental tools used to extract physical properties of hadrons, such as masses and decay constants, by studying their time evolution. These correlators are calculated from the vacuum expectation values of carefully chosen operators that encode the interactive dynamics of quarks and gluons in a non-perturbative framework.

Construction of Correlators

The two-point correlation function for a hadron is given by:

C(t)=∑x⟨0|O(x,t)O†(0,0)|0⟩,C(t)=∑x⟨0|O(x,t)O†(0,0)|0⟩,

where O(x,t)O(x,t) is an interpolating operator with the same quantum numbers as the hadron of interest.

Mesonic Operators

For mesons, O(x,t)=ψ¯1(x,t)Γψ2(x,t)O(x,t)=ψ¯1(x,t)Γψ2(x,t), where ΓΓ determines the quantum numbers:

  • Pseudoscalar mesons (e.g., B0B0, D0D0, pion, ηcηc): Γ=γ5Γ=γ5,
  • Vector mesons (e.g., ρρ, D∗D∗): Γ=γiΓ=γi (where i=x,y,zi=x,y,z).

The quark content depends on the meson in question. For example pion (π+π+) is ud¯ud¯, D0D0 being cu¯cu¯ and ηcηc: being cc¯cc¯ :

Baryonic Operators

For baryons, the operators are more complex due to their three-quark structure. The standard interpolating operators for nucleons, denoted as N1/2+1N11/2+ and N1/2+2N21/2+, are given by:

N1/2+1=ϵijk(uTiCγ5dj)uk,N11/2+=ϵijk(uiTCγ5dj)uk,

N1/2+2=ϵijk(uTiCdj)γ5uk,N21/2+=ϵijk(uiTCdj)γ5uk,

where:

  • ϵijkϵijk ensures antisymmetry in the color indices, enforcing color neutrality.
  • CC is the charge conjugation matrix, which ensures the correct symmetry of the spinor fields.
  • γ5γ5 provides the appropriate spin-parity quantum numbers for the nucleon (( J^P = 1/2^+ )).

Heavy Baryons

The same structure is used to construct operators for heavy baryons by substituting the light quarks (uu, dd) with heavy quarks (cc, ss, bb). For example:

  • Cascade (Ξ0Ξ0, uscusc): OΞ0c=ϵijk(uTiCγ5sj)ckOΞc0=ϵijk(uiTCγ5sj)ck
  • Lambda (Λ+cΛc+, udcudc): OΛ+c=ϵijk(uTiCγ5dj)ckOΛc+=ϵijk(uiTCγ5dj)ck
  • Sigma (Σ+cΣc+, uucuuc): OΣ+c=ϵijk(uTiCγ5uj)ckOΣc+=ϵijk(uiTCγ5uj)ck

Nucleon Correlator

The nucleon two-point correlation function is constructed by creating a nucleon at the origin and destroying it at a later time ( t ). This is expressed as:

C±(t)=∑x⟨0|N(x,t)(1±γ4)N¯(0,0)|0⟩,C±(t)=∑x⟨0|N(x,t)(1±γ4)N¯(0,0)|0⟩,

where:

  • N(x,t)N(x,t) is the interpolating operator for the nucleon.
  • (1±γ4)(1±γ4) is a projection operator, isolating contributions from positive and negative parity states.

The specific representation of the correlator separates contributions from the lightest positive and negative parity states:

C+(t)→A+e−m+t+A−e−m−(T−t),C+(t)→A+e−m+t+A−e−m−(T−t),

C−(t)→A−e−m−t+A+e−m+(T−t),C−(t)→A−e−m−t+A+e−m+(T−t),

where:

  • m+m+ and m−m− are the masses of the lightest positive and negative parity states, respectively.
  • TT is the temporal extent of the lattice.

Ground State Energy and Effective Mass

The Euclidean time dependence of C(t)C(t) reveals the spectrum of the system:

C(t)=∑n=0∞Ane−Ent,C(t)=∑n=0∞Ane−Ent,

where EnEn are the energy levels, AnAn are the overlaps of OO with the states, and the ground state energy E0E0 dominates as t→∞t→∞. Approximating the above equation by using large values of tt reduces the equation to a single exponential.

One way to use the correlator values is to use the \textit{effective mass plot}. The effective mass is defined as:

meff=log(C(t)C(t+1))meff=log⁡(C(t)C(t+1))

where C(t)C(t) is the two-point correlation function at time tt. This is only true when we take a single exponential as the fitting function and ignore the small values of tt.

Although the above method might seem quite fool-proof in terms of getting the ground state energy and effective masses using only large values of tt, the main disadvantage comes from the excessive noise at large time steps, making the calculated parameters less precise and noisy.

To prevent the above problem, the fit is done using the “Window Method”, i.e., selecting a certain range of tt. Despite this, often the noise is too excessive and the window is almost absent, and no plateau is observed at all, making any sort of fit unreliable.

Extracting Ground State Energy using Single Exponential Fitting

We can write the two-point correlator function as discussed earlier and approximate it as

C(t)=∑n=0∞Ane−Ent∼A0e−E0tC(t)=∑n=0∞Ane−Ent∼A0e−E0t

The above form has a closed form solution when linearised such that:
log(C(t))=log(A0)−E0∗tlog⁡(C(t))=log⁡(A0)−E0∗t

The parameters can be estimated using a simple Least Square Fitting formula where we minimize the error function, denoted as χ2χ2 (or SS), which represents the sum of the squared differences between the observed logarithmic data and the linear model.

This error function is defined as:

χ2=∑i=1N[log(C(ti))−(log(A0)−E0ti)]2χ2=∑i=1N[log⁡(C(ti))−(log⁡(A0)−E0ti)]2

To determine the optimal parameters, we minimize χ2χ2 by requiring that the partial derivatives with respect to the slope (−E0−E0) and the intercept (logA0log⁡A0) vanish:

∂χ2∂E0=0and∂χ2∂(logA0)=0∂χ2∂E0=0and∂χ2∂(log⁡A0)=0

Solving this system of simultaneous linear equations yields the analytic solution for E0E0:

Window Method

Given the analytic form for the two point correlator functions consists of mutiple different exponential terms we simply cannot assume it to be consisting of a single decay mode only making the estimation of E0E0 using the Single Exponential Fitting Technique less accurate. To address this specific problem we look into the window method.

  • For the correlator of the form C(t)=∑∞n=0Ane−EntC(t)=∑n=0∞Ane−Ent we can write estimate the ground state energy by fitting a single exponential function given that we modify our data such that we start fitting from a specific T=t0T=t0 such that e−Ei∗t0→0∀i≠0e−Ei∗t0→0∀i≠0.
  • As one moves towards larger values of tt, the estimation of C(t)C(t) tends to become noisy due to precision errors. For very large values of tt the value of C(t)C(t) tends to become noisy making the estimation of ground state again difficuly, hence we modify our data such that we only fit our correlator values only for valuse of t≤t1t≤t1 where the correlator do not suffer from the presence of simulation noise.

For the values of t∈[t0,t1]t∈[t0,t1] when a single exponential function is fitted to given correlator we observe a region of plateau. We estimate our ground state energy value using the E0E0 value obtained in this plateau.

The above figure represents the estimation of E0E0 using the window method. The window is represented as the grey shaded portion were we can roughly observe some degree of saturation. One thing to be noted from the above figure is the difference in position and width of the plateau region. This is primarily due to the fact that different states have different excited energy values. Smaller excited energy levels need larger values of tt to reach complete saturation whereas some states are indeed noisy due to simulation noise reducing the width of the saturation region further. This makes the window method unrealiable in practice. Moreover there does not exist any gurantee of existence of such a saturation region making the estimation of ground state further complicated.

Extracting Ground State Energy using Double Exponential Fitting

We now try to extend our analysis though the inclusion of the first excited state in the fitting function itself, the correlator therefore takes the following form.

C(t)=∑n=0∞Ane−Ent∼A0e−E0t+A1e−E1tC(t)=∑n=0∞Ane−Ent∼A0e−E0t+A1e−E1t

In the last section we described the issue of using the window method and how it fails when the saturation region for t∈[t0,t1]t∈[t0,t1] shrink. There exists only two ways to solve this issue:

  • Increase the precision of the simulation: By increasing the simulation precision we make sure that the inversion errors are minimized. This makes sure that the region of saturation increases by pushing t1t1 further to right. Eventhough this sounds promising the computational cost that it brings with it makes it impossible.
  • Fitting multiple exponentials: This makes it possbile to absorb the higher order energy states into the fitting function. This makes it possible push the t0t0 boundary further towards left.

In this work we would mainly explore how we can push the t1t1 boundary further towards the left making the plateau region wider and the estimation of E0E0 more accurate.

As established, the double exponential model C(t)≈A0e−E0t+A1e−E1tC(t)≈A0e−E0t+A1e−E1t cannot be linearized via logarithmic transformation due to the additive nature of the exponential terms. The parameter estimation now becomes a non-linear least square fitting problem.

Let θ=[A0,E0,A1,E1]⊤θ=[A0,E0,A1,E1]⊤ be the vector of parameters we want to estimate. We define the residual vector r(θ)r(θ) comprising the differences between the observed correlator data yi=C(ti)yi=C(ti) and the model prediction f(ti,θ)f(ti,θ).

The objective is to minimize the cost function S(θ)S(θ), defined as:

S(θ)=12∥r(θ)∥2=12∑i=1N(yi−f(ti,θ))2S(θ)=12‖r(θ)‖2=12∑i=1N(yi−f(ti,θ))2

We estimate the gradients corresponding to the energy parameters EnEn (where n∈{0,1}n∈{0,1}), we apply the chain rule to the cost function S(θ)S(θ):

∂S∂En=∑i=1N(yi−f(ti,θ))∂∂En(yi−f(ti,θ))∂S∂En=∑i=1N(yi−f(ti,θ))∂∂En(yi−f(ti,θ))

Recalling that the model is defined as f(t)=∑kAke−Ektf(t)=∑kAke−Ekt, the partial derivative of the model function with respect to the energy EnEn is:

∂f(ti)∂En=−tiAne−Enti∂f(ti)∂En=−tiAne−Enti

Substituting this result back into the gradient equation yields the specific update term for the energy:

∂S∂En=∑i=1NtiAne−Enti(yi−f(ti,θ))∂S∂En=∑i=1NtiAne−Enti(yi−f(ti,θ))

Further simplifying the equational form:

∂S∂En=∑i=1Nti∗yi∗r(θ)∂S∂En=∑i=1Nti∗yi∗r(θ)

The value of ∂S∂En∂S∂En decays rapidly due to the exponential nature of C(t)C(t) making the gradient to decay to zero for larger values of tt.

To address the issue of vanishing gradient discussed above can modify our residual such that

Slog(θ)=12∑i=1N(log(yi)−log(f(ti,θ)))2Slog(θ)=12∑i=1N(log⁡(yi)−log⁡(f(ti,θ)))2

Differentiating with respect to the energy parameter EnEn:

∂Slog∂En=∑i=1N(log(yi)−log(f(ti)))∂∂En(−log(f(ti)))∂Slog∂En=∑i=1N(log⁡(yi)−log⁡(f(ti)))∂∂En(−log⁡(f(ti)))

Using the chain rule ∂log(u)∂x=1u∂u∂x∂log⁡(u)∂x=1u∂u∂x, and substituting the derivative of the model function:

∂∂Enlog(f(ti))=1f(ti)(−tiAne−Enti)∂∂Enlog⁡(f(ti))=1f(ti)(−tiAne−Enti)

The final gradient expression becomes:

∂Slog∂En=∑i=1Nti(Ane−Entif(ti))(log(yi)−log(f(ti)))∂Slog∂En=∑i=1Nti(Ane−Entif(ti))(log⁡(yi)−log⁡(f(ti)))

The term (Ane−Entif(ti))(Ane−Entif(ti)) now no longer decay exponentially preventing the gradients from vanishing to zero.

The loglog modification can be simply extedended for any existing least square-fitting technique by regressing over the loglog of predicted and true values.

Gauss-Newton algorithm

Setting ∇S(θ)=0∇S(θ)=0 yields a system of non-linear equations with no closed-form solution. We therefore proceed iteratively, updating the parameters by a step size δδ such that:

θk+1=θk+δθk+1=θk+δ

The δδ can be estimated using the Gauss-Newton normal equations:

(J⊤J)δ=J⊤r(J⊤J)δ=J⊤r

where JJ is the Jacobian matrix of size N×4N×4, containing the partial derivatives of the model with respect to each parameter. The matrix J⊤JJ⊤J acts as an approximation to the Hessian matrix.

The efficiency of the Gauss-Newton algorithm is heavily dependent on the proximity of the initial guess to the true solution and the magnitude of the residuals.

Under ideal conditions, where the initial parameter estimate θ0θ0 is close to the global minimum θ∗θ∗, the Gauss-Newton method shows a quadratic convergence rate. This implies that the error at step k+1k+1 is proportional to the square of the error at step kk:

∥θk+1−θ∗∥≤C∥θk−θ∗∥2‖θk+1−θ∗‖≤C‖θk−θ∗‖2

This rapid convergence arises because the matrix J⊤JJ⊤J serves as a strong approximator of the true Hessian matrix HH. The full Hessian of the squared error function S(θ)S(θ) is given by:

H=∇2S(θ)=J⊤J+∑i=1Nri(θ)∇2ri(θ)H=∇2S(θ)=J⊤J+∑i=1Nri(θ)∇2ri(θ)

The Gauss-Newton method ignores the second term, ∑ri∇2ri∑ri∇2ri. This approximation holds valid when the residuals riri are small. If the initial guess θ0θ0 is far from the minimum Hessian is poorly estimated leading to divergence.

Gradient Descent Algorithm

Gauss-Newton suffers from the problem of poor convergence when the initial guess values are far from the minma of the loss-landscape. The Gradient Descent (GD) algorithm is known to be robust towards such situation given it relies on first-order information and can converge to a good solution even with a poor initial guess value.

To minimize the cost function S(θ)S(θ), Gradient Descent iteratively updates the parameters by taking steps proportional to the negative of the gradient of the function at the current point. The update rule is given by:

θk+1=θk−α∇S(θk)θk+1=θk−α∇S(θk)

where αα is a positive scalar known as the learning rate (step size), which controls the magnitude of the update.

Following our matrix notation from the previous sections the gradient can be expressed as

∇S(θ)=−J⊤r∇S(θ)=−J⊤r

Thus, the update step becomes:

θk+1=θk+αJ⊤rθk+1=θk+αJ⊤r

Gradient Descent exhibits linear convergence but close to the minimum it is significantly slower than the quadratic convergence of Gauss-Newton. The Gradient Descent algorithm suffers from oscillations within the narrow valleys of the loss landscape when the parameters approach the minima, which consequently leads to very slow convergence.

Levenberg-Marquardt Algorithm

The Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm introduces a damping parameter λλ to regularize the Hessian approximation. The update equation is modified to:

(J⊤J+λI)δ=J⊤r(J⊤J+λI)δ=J⊤r

where II is the identity matrix. Addition of this dampening parameter makes it possible to combine the best of both the Gauss-Newton and Gradient-Descent algorithm into a single update step.The parameter λλ acts as a toggle between two optimization regimes:

  • High λλ: The term λIλI dominates. The equation approximates λδ=J⊤r=−∇Sλδ=J⊤r=−∇S, essentially performing Gradient Descent. This is robust when the current guess is far from the minimum but converges slowly.
  • Low λλ: The term J⊤JJ⊤J dominates. The method approaches Gauss-Newton, providing rapid quadratic convergence near the minimum.

Gradient Descent and Annealing

Now we try to completely replace the Gauss-Newton component of the optimisation algorithm to make it purely Gradient Descent (GD) based. The reasoning behind this can be established from the ill-conditioned nature of the approximate Hessian matrix J⊤JJ⊤J which leads to instabilities. Building on the intuition from the previous section we can say that a pure GD based algorithm works very well when it is away from the local minima but oscillates when it reaches the minimum. To solve this we can tune the αα parameter for the update step. This can be done in a few ways:

  • Small αα: By setting the value of αα sufficiently small we can ensure that the algorithm does not oscillate a lot near the optima. This do comes with the cost of having extremely slow convergence away from the minimal and the possibility of the algorithm to get stuck in local minimas or saddle points.

  • Decaying αα: The optimisation set can start with sufficiently large value of αα and as the parameters converge the αα can be decayed to prevent slow convergences. The decaying of αα does not guarantee that the optimisation would not be trapped in a local minima.

  • Annealing αα: To address the limitations of monotonic decay, we employ a cyclic scheduling strategy, specifically Cosine Annealing with Warm Restarts (SGDR). In this approach, the learning rate follows a periodic cosine function that decays to a minimum value and then abruptly resets (restarts) to its initial maximum.

    Mathematically, within the ii-th run (or cycle), the learning rate αtαt at step tt is updated as:

    αt=αimin+12(αimax−αimin)(1+cos(TcurTiπ))αt=αmini+12(αmaxi−αmini)(1+cos⁡(TcurTiπ))

    where αminαmin and αmaxαmax are the range of the learning rate, TcurTcur is the number of epochs since the last restart, and TiTi is the number of epochs in the current cycle. This stategic annealing process with restarts prevents the optimisation algorithm from getting stuck in local minima while still ensuring that it converges strongly with minimal osciallations.

Given that we are doing a double exponential fitting task and there exists a strong potential for collapse where a the fitting function A0e−E0t+A1e−E1tA0e−E0t+A1e−E1t trivially collapses to a single exponential by one of the following modes:

  • Ai→0Ai→0: One of the coefficents become zero reducing the fitting function to a trivial single expoenential
  • E0∼E1E0∼E1: Both the energy values come very close to each other making the exponential term similar causing collapse.
  • Ei>>EjEi>>Ej: One of the energy term blows up pushing the negative exponential towards zero.

We evaluated using both annealing type case and a linear learning rate regime and found that even if annealing is promising on paper it seldom helps in this use case. The overall error fluctuation in the ground state energy for different cut indices do seem to reduce drastically.

Neural Guessing and Levenberg-Marquardt Algorithm

We saw in the previous section how the GD based approach even when assited with techniques such as annealing and restarts fail to discover the solutions to our fitting problem. To better understand we look into the loss landscape for the different parameter values of a given state. Given that our parameter set that we want to estimate is of the form θ=[A0,E0,A1,E1]θ=[A0,E0,A1,E1] and has four independent variables that primarily dominate the loss landscape we segregate the parameters into the sets of coefficients (AiAi) and exponents (EiEi). One thing to note is AiAis can be trivially calculated using the normal form equation of a linear regression if EiEis for a system is known. We exploit this property to calculate the minimum error that can be achieved by your fitting function for our data for any given set of exponents/energies. We plot this variation of error for different energy sets to discover the nature of the loss landscape. For this specific demonstration we chose B0_st as our state of choice.

The above figure shows the distribution of error (as colormap) for different values of E0E0 and E1E1. To zoom into the distribution we use loglog transformation on the error axis. The colorbar represents the mapping of the colors into numeric error value for the given transformation (for the loglog plot the colorbar maps the log(Error)log(Error)). As can be seen from the above plots that the lose surface suffers predominantly from extremely narrow and steep valley and long plateau regions where gradients disapper.

The very nature of the loss landscape caused GD based optimisation steps to violently oscilate near the solution region and stall in the plateau regions. To solve this problem we introduce the Neural Guessing Based Levenberg-Marquardt Algorithm (LMA-NG). We have already discussed how LMA acts like Gauss Newton (GN) algorithm when provided with a strong guess value and the quadratic convergence rate of the GN algorithm. Here we try to train a neural network on the exisiting data such that it accurate models the solution region (parametric regions that are very close to the global minima) such that elements from the said region can act as strong guess value candidate for a Guess-Newton or LMA type optimisation algorithm.

The above diagram represent the schematics of the Neural Network component of the LMA-NG algorithm. The input to the network is an NN-dimensional noise vector z∈RNz∈RN (where N=64N=64), sampled from a standard multivariate normal distribution:

z∼N(0,IN)z∼N(0,IN)

This vector propagates through a sequence of hidden layers, each performing a composite operation consisting of an affine transformation followed by a non-linear activation.
The Linear Layers perform an affine transformation defined as:

h(k)=W(k)h(k−1)+b(k)h(k)=W(k)h(k−1)+b(k)

where W(k)W(k) and b(k)b(k) are learnable parameters. The output of each linear layer is passed through a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function, defined element-wise as:
ReLU(x)=max(0,x)ReLU(x)=max(0,x)
 The model finally outputs the associated guess values as AiAis and EiEis after passing the input noise through all the non-linear transformations. The following losses are used to train the neural network:
Lfit=1N∑i=1N[log(C(ti))−log(Ae−E0ti+Be−E1ti+ϵ)]2Lfit=1N∑i=1N[log⁡(C(ti))−log⁡(Ae−E0ti+Be−E1ti+ϵ)]2

Ldiv=∑k=01(max(0,1−σ(Ek))+max(0,1−σ(Ak)))Ldiv=∑k=01(max(0,1−σ(Ek))+max(0,1−σ(Ak)))

where ϵϵ is a small constant for numerical stability.

Ltotal=Lfit+LdivLtotal=Lfit+Ldiv

The fitting loss, LfitLfit ensures that the neural network learns the region of parameter space where the fitting error is minimum for a double exponential fit. The diversity regularization term, LdivLdiv, prevents mode collapse by enforcing a minimum variance in the predicted parameters across the batch. It is formulated using a hinge loss that penalizes the standard deviation σσ of the amplitudes AA and energies EE if they fall below a unit margin. This term encourages the network to explore the parameter space and maintain a diverse distribution of solutions.
