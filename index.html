Heavy Spectral Decomposition for Noisy Correlators in LQCD using ML

Introduction

QCD is hard to solve when low-energy states are taken into account. Given the inability to use perturbative methods in QCD in low-energy states, the most popular method to understand the interaction of quarks and gluons is through the use of lattice QCD, where QCD is embedded onto a lattice or the continuum is discretized into intervals and the resultant equations are calculated numerically.

Correlators in Lattice QCD

In lattice QCD, correlators \cite{McNeile_2004} are fundamental tools used to extract physical properties of hadrons, such as masses and decay constants, by studying their time evolution. These correlators are calculated from the vacuum expectation values of carefully chosen operators that encode the interactive dynamics of quarks and gluons in a non-perturbative framework.

Construction of Correlators

The two-point correlation function for a hadron is given by:

$$ C(t) = \sum_{\mathbf{x}} \langle 0 | O(\mathbf{x}, t) O^\dagger(0, 0) | 0 \rangle, $$

where $O(\mathbf{x}, t)$ is an interpolating operator with the same quantum numbers as the hadron of interest.

Mesonic Operators

For mesons, $O(\mathbf{x}, t) = \bar{\psi}_1(\mathbf{x}, t) \Gamma \psi_2(\mathbf{x}, t)$, where $\Gamma$ determines the quantum numbers:

  • Pseudoscalar mesons (e.g., $B^0$, $D^0$, pion, $\eta_c$): $\Gamma = \gamma_5$,
  • Vector mesons (e.g., $\rho$, $D^*$): $\Gamma = \gamma_i$ (where $i = x, y, z$).

The quark content depends on the meson in question. For example pion ($\pi^+$) is $u \bar{d}$, $D^0$ being $c \bar{u}$ and $\eta_c$: being $c \bar{c}$.

Baryonic Operators

For baryons, the operators are more complex due to their three-quark structure. The standard interpolating operators for nucleons, denoted as $N_1^{1/2+}$ and $N_2^{1/2+}$, are given by:

$$ N_1^{1/2+} = \epsilon_{ijk} \left(u_i^T C \gamma_5 d_j\right) u_k, $$ $$ N_2^{1/2+} = \epsilon_{ijk} \left(u_i^T C d_j\right) \gamma_5 u_k, $$

where:

  • $\epsilon_{ijk}$ ensures antisymmetry in the color indices, enforcing color neutrality.
  • $C$ is the charge conjugation matrix, which ensures the correct symmetry of the spinor fields.
  • $\gamma_5$ provides the appropriate spin-parity quantum numbers for the nucleon ($J^P = 1/2^+$).

Heavy Baryons

The same structure is used to construct operators for heavy baryons by substituting the light quarks ($u$, $d$) with heavy quarks ($c$, $s$, $b$). For example:

  • Cascade ($\Xi^0$, $usc$): $O_{\Xi_c^0} = \epsilon_{ijk} \left(u_i^T C \gamma_5 s_j\right) c_k$
  • Lambda ($\Lambda_c^+$, $udc$): $O_{\Lambda_c^+} = \epsilon_{ijk} \left(u_i^T C \gamma_5 d_j\right) c_k$
  • Sigma ($\Sigma_c^+$, $uuc$): $O_{\Sigma_c^+} = \epsilon_{ijk} \left(u_i^T C \gamma_5 u_j\right) c_k$

Nucleon Correlator

The nucleon two-point correlation function is constructed by creating a nucleon at the origin and destroying it at a later time $t$. This is expressed as:

$$ C_\pm(t) = \sum_{\mathbf{x}} \langle 0 | N(\mathbf{x}, t) (1 \pm \gamma_4) \bar{N}(0, 0) | 0 \rangle, $$

where:

  • $N(\mathbf{x}, t)$ is the interpolating operator for the nucleon.
  • $(1 \pm \gamma_4)$ is a projection operator, isolating contributions from positive and negative parity states.

The specific representation of the correlator separates contributions from the lightest positive and negative parity states:

$$ C_+(t) \rightarrow A^+ e^{-m_+ t} + A^- e^{-m_-(T-t)}, $$ $$ C_-(t) \rightarrow A^- e^{-m_- t} + A^+ e^{-m_+(T-t)}, $$

where:

  • $m_+$ and $m_-$ are the masses of the lightest positive and negative parity states, respectively.
  • $T$ is the temporal extent of the lattice.

Ground State Energy and Effective Mass

The Euclidean time dependence of $C(t)$ reveals the spectrum of the system:

$$ C(t) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty A_n e^{-E_n t}, $$

where $E_n$ are the energy levels, $A_n$ are the overlaps of $O$ with the states, and the ground state energy $E_0$ dominates as $t \to \infty$. Approximating the above equation by using large values of $t$ reduces the equation to a single exponential.

One way to use the correlator values is to use the effective mass plot. The effective mass is defined as:

$$ m_{\text{eff}} = \log\left(\frac{C(t)}{C(t+1)}\right) $$

where $C(t)$ is the two-point correlation function at time $t$. This is only true when we take a single exponential as the fitting function and ignore the small values of $t$.

Although the above method might seem quite fool-proof in terms of getting the ground state energy and effective masses using only large values of $t$, the main disadvantage comes from the excessive noise at large time steps, making the calculated parameters less precise and noisy.

To prevent the above problem, the fit is done using the "Window Method", i.e., selecting a certain range of $t$. Despite this, often the noise is too excessive and the window is almost absent, and no plateau is observed at all, making any sort of fit unreliable.

Extracting Ground State Energy using Single Exponential Fitting

We can write the two-point correlator function as discussed earlier and approximate it as

$$ C(t) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty A_n e^{-E_n t} \sim A_0 e^{-E_0 t} $$

The above form has a closed form solution when linearised such that:

$$ \log(C(t)) = \log(A_0) - E_0*t $$

The parameters can be estimated using a simple Least Square Fitting formula where we minimize the error function, denoted as $\chi^2$ (or $S$), which represents the sum of the squared differences between the observed logarithmic data and the linear model.

This error function is defined as:

$$ \chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ \log(C(t_i)) - (\log(A_0) - E_0 t_i) \right]^2 $$

To determine the optimal parameters, we minimize $\chi^2$ by requiring that the partial derivatives with respect to the slope ($-E_0$) and the intercept ($\log A_0$) vanish:

$$ \frac{\partial \chi^2}{\partial E_0} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial \chi^2}{\partial (\log A_0)} = 0 $$

Solving this system of simultaneous linear equations yields the analytic solution for $E_0$:

$$ E_0 = \frac{(\sum t_i)(\sum \log(C(t_i))) - N \sum (t_i \cdot \log(C(t_i)))}{N \sum (t_i^2) - (\sum t_i)^2} $$

Window Method

Given the analytic form for the two point correlator functions consists of mutiple different exponential terms we simply cannot assume it to be consisting of a single decay mode only making the estimation of $E_0$ using the Single Exponential Fitting Technique less accurate. To address this specific problem we look into the window method.

  • For the correlator of the form $C(t) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty A_n e^{-E_n t}$ we can write estimate the ground state energy by fitting a single exponential function given that we modify our data such that we start fitting from a specific $T=t_0$ such that $e^{-E_i*t_0} \rightarrow 0 \,\,\,\, \forall i \ne0$.
  • As one moves towards larger values of $t$, the estimation of $C(t)$ tends to become noisy due to precision errors. For very large values of $t$ the value of $C(t)$ tends to become noisy making the estimation of ground state again difficuly, hence we modify our data such that we only fit our correlator values only for valuse of $t \le t_1$ where the correlator do not suffer from the presence of simulation noise.

For the values of $t \in [t_0,t_1]$ when a single exponential function is fitted to given correlator we observe a region of plateau. We estimate our ground state energy value using the $E_0$ value obtained in this plateau.

The above figure represents the estimation of $E_0$ using the window method. The window is represented as the grey shaded portion were we can roughly observe some degree of saturation. One thing to be noted from the above figure is the difference in position and width of the plateau region. This is primarily due to the fact that different states have different excited energy values. Smaller excited energy levels need larger values of $t$ to reach complete saturation whereas some states are indeed noisy due to simulation noise reducing the width of the saturation region further. This makes the window method unrealiable in practice. Moreover there does not exist any gurantee of existence of such a saturation region making the estimation of ground state further complicated.

Extracting Ground State Energy using Double Exponential Fitting

We now try to extend our analysis though the inclusion of the first excited state in the fitting function itself, the correlator therefore takes the following form.

$$ C(t) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty A_n e^{-E_n t} \sim A_0 e^{-E_0 t} + A_1 e^{-E_1 t} $$

In the last section we described the issue of using the window method and how it fails when the saturation region for $t \in [t_0,t_1]$ shrink. There exists only two ways to solve this issue:

  • Increase the precision of the simulation: By increasing the simulation precision we make sure that the inversion errors are minimized. This makes sure that the region of saturation increases by pushing $t_1$ further to right. Eventhough this sounds promising the computational cost that it brings with it makes it impossible.
  • Fitting multiple exponentials: This makes it possbile to absorb the higher order energy states into the fitting function. This makes it possible push the $t_0$ boundary further towards left.

In this work we would mainly explore how we can push the $t_1$ boundary further towards the left making the plateau region wider and the estimation of $E_0$ more accurate.

As established, the double exponential model $C(t) \approx A_0 e^{-E_0 t} + A_1 e^{-E_1 t}$ cannot be linearized via logarithmic transformation due to the additive nature of the exponential terms. The parameter estimation now becomes a non-linear least square fitting problem.

Let $\boldsymbol{\theta} = [A_0, E_0, A_1, E_1]^\top$ be the vector of parameters we want to estimate. We define the residual vector $\mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ comprising the differences between the observed correlator data $y_i = C(t_i)$ and the model prediction $f(t_i, \boldsymbol{\theta})$.

The objective is to minimize the cost function $S(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, defined as:

$$ S(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2} \| \mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \|^2 = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( y_i - f(t_i, \boldsymbol{\theta}) \right)^2 $$

We estimate the gradients corresponding to the energy parameters $E_n$ (where $n \in \{0, 1\}$), we apply the chain rule to the cost function $S(\boldsymbol{\theta})$:

$$ \frac{\partial S}{\partial E_n} = \sum_{i=1}^N \left( y_i - f(t_i, \boldsymbol{\theta}) \right) \frac{\partial}{\partial E_n} \left( y_i - f(t_i, \boldsymbol{\theta}) \right) $$

Recalling that the model is defined as $f(t) = \sum_k A_k e^{-E_k t}$, the partial derivative of the model function with respect to the energy $E_n$ is:

$$ \frac{\partial f(t_i)}{\partial E_n} = -t_i A_n e^{-E_n t_i} $$

Substituting this result back into the gradient equation yields the specific update term for the energy:

$$ \frac{\partial S}{\partial E_n} = \sum_{i=1}^N t_i A_n e^{-E_n t_i} \left( y_i - f(t_i, \boldsymbol{\theta}) \right) $$

Further simplifying the equational form:

$$ \frac{\partial S}{\partial E_n} = \sum_{i=1}^N t_i*y_i*\mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) $$

The value of $\frac{\partial S}{\partial E_n}$ decays rapidly due to the exponential nature of $C(t)$ making the gradient to decay to zero for larger values of $t$.

To address the issue of vanishing gradient discussed above can modify our residual such that

$$ S_{\log}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( \log(y_i) - \log(f(t_i, \boldsymbol{\theta})) \right)^2 $$

Differentiating with respect to the energy parameter $E_n$:

$$ \frac{\partial S_{\log}}{\partial E_n} = \sum_{i=1}^N \left( \log(y_i) - \log(f(t_i)) \right) \frac{\partial}{\partial E_n} \left( - \log(f(t_i)) \right) $$

Using the chain rule $\frac{\partial \log(u)}{\partial x} = \frac{1}{u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$, and substituting the derivative of the model function:

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial E_n} \log(f(t_i)) = \frac{1}{f(t_i)} (-t_i A_n e^{-E_n t_i}) $$

The final gradient expression becomes:

$$ \frac{\partial S_{\log}}{\partial E_n} = \sum_{i=1}^N t_i \left( \frac{A_n e^{-E_n t_i}}{f(t_i)} \right) \left( \log(y_i) - \log(f(t_i)) \right) $$

The term $\left( \frac{A_n e^{-E_n t_i}}{f(t_i)} \right)$ now no longer decay exponentially preventing the gradients from vanishing to zero.

The $\log$ modification can be simply extedended for any existing least square-fitting technique by regressing over the $\log$ of predicted and true values.

Gauss-Newton algorithm

Setting $\nabla S(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = 0$ yields a system of non-linear equations with no closed-form solution. We therefore proceed iteratively, updating the parameters by a step size $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ such that:

$$ \boldsymbol{\theta}_{k+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_k + \boldsymbol{\delta} $$

The $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ can be estimated using the Gauss-Newton normal equations:

$$ (\mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{J}) \boldsymbol{\delta} = \mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{r} $$

where $\mathbf{J}$ is the Jacobian matrix of size $N \times 4$, containing the partial derivatives of the model with respect to each parameter. The matrix $\mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{J}$ acts as an approximation to the Hessian matrix.

The efficiency of the Gauss-Newton algorithm is heavily dependent on the proximity of the initial guess to the true solution and the magnitude of the residuals.

Under ideal conditions, where the initial parameter estimate $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$ is close to the global minimum $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$, the Gauss-Newton method shows a quadratic convergence rate. This implies that the error at step $k+1$ is proportional to the square of the error at step $k$:

$$ \| \boldsymbol{\theta}_{k+1} - \boldsymbol{\theta}^* \| \le C \| \boldsymbol{\theta}_k - \boldsymbol{\theta}^* \|^2 $$

This rapid convergence arises because the matrix $\mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{J}$ serves as a strong approximator of the true Hessian matrix $\mathbf{H}$. The full Hessian of the squared error function $S(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is given by:

$$ \mathbf{H} = \nabla^2 S(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{J} + \sum_{i=1}^N r_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \nabla^2 r_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}) $$

The Gauss-Newton method ignores the second term, $\sum r_i \nabla^2 r_i$. This approximation holds valid when the residuals $r_i$ are small. If the initial guess $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$ is far from the minimum Hessian is poorly estimated leading to divergence.

Gradient Descent Algorithm

Gauss-Newton suffers from the problem of poor convergence when the initial guess values are far from the minma of the loss-landscape. The Gradient Descent (GD) algorithm is known to be robust towards such situation given it relies on first-order information and can converge to a good solution even with a poor initial guess value.

To minimize the cost function $S(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, Gradient Descent iteratively updates the parameters by taking steps proportional to the negative of the gradient of the function at the current point. The update rule is given by:

$$ \boldsymbol{\theta}_{k+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_k - \alpha \nabla S(\boldsymbol{\theta}_k) $$

where $\alpha$ is a positive scalar known as the learning rate (step size), which controls the magnitude of the update.

Following our matrix notation from the previous sections the gradient can be expressed as

$$ \nabla S(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = - \mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{r} $$

Thus, the update step becomes:

$$ \boldsymbol{\theta}_{k+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_k + \alpha \mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{r} $$

Gradient Descent exhibits linear convergence but close to the minimum it is significantly slower than the quadratic convergence of Gauss-Newton. The Gradient Descent algorithm suffers from oscillations within the narrow valleys of the loss landscape when the parameters approach the minima, which consequently leads to very slow convergence.

Levenberg-Marquardt Algorithm

The Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm introduces a damping parameter $\lambda$ to regularize the Hessian approximation. The update equation is modified to:

$$ (\mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{J} + \lambda \mathbf{I}) \boldsymbol{\delta} = \mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{r} $$

where $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity matrix. Addition of this dampening parameter makes it possible to combine the best of both the Gauss-Newton and Gradient-Descent algorithm into a single update step.The parameter $\lambda$ acts as a toggle between two optimization regimes:

  • High $\lambda$: The term $\lambda \mathbf{I}$ dominates. The equation approximates $\lambda \boldsymbol{\delta} = \mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{r} = -\nabla S$, essentially performing Gradient Descent. This is robust when the current guess is far from the minimum but converges slowly.
  • Low $\lambda$: The term $\mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{J}$ dominates. The method approaches Gauss-Newton, providing rapid quadratic convergence near the minimum.

Gradient Descent and Annealing

Now we try to completely replace the Gauss-Newton component of the optimisation algorithm to make it purely Gradient Descent (GD) based. The reasoning behind this can be established from the ill-conditioned nature of the approximate Hessian matrix $\mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{J}$ which leads to instabilities. Building on the intuition from the previous section we can say that a pure GD based algorithm works very well when it is away from the local minima but oscillates when it reaches the minimum. To solve this we can tune the $\alpha$ parameter for the update step. This can be done in a few ways:

  • Small $\alpha$: By setting the value of $\alpha$ sufficiently small we can ensure that the algorithm does not oscillate a lot near the optima. This do comes with the cost of having extremely slow convergence away from the minimal and the possibility of the algorithm to get stuck in local minimas or saddle points.
  • Decaying $\alpha$: The optimisation set can start with sufficiently large value of $\alpha$ and as the parameters converge the $\alpha$ can be decayed to prevent slow convergences. The decaying of $\alpha$ does not guarantee that the optimisation would not be trapped in a local minima.
  • Annealing $\alpha$: To address the limitations of monotonic decay, we employ a cyclic scheduling strategy, specifically Cosine Annealing with Warm Restarts (SGDR). In this approach, the learning rate follows a periodic cosine function that decays to a minimum value and then abruptly resets (restarts) to its initial maximum.

Mathematically, within the $i$-th run (or cycle), the learning rate $\alpha_t$ at step $t$ is updated as:

$$ \alpha_t = \alpha_{\min}^i + \frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{\max}^i - \alpha_{\min}^i) \left( 1 + \cos \left( \frac{T_{cur}}{T_i} \pi \right) \right) $$

where $\alpha_{\min}$ and $\alpha_{\max}$ are the range of the learning rate, $T_{cur}$ is the number of epochs since the last restart, and $T_i$ is the number of epochs in the current cycle. This stategic annealing process with restarts prevents the optimisation algorithm from getting stuck in local minima while still ensuring that it converges strongly with minimal osciallations.

In the above plot we show the groud state energy values for different cut indices. Given that we are doing a double exponential fitting task and there exists a strong potential for collapse where a the fitting function $A_0 e^{-E_0 t} + A_1 e^{-E_1 t}$ trivially collapses to a single exponential by one of the following modes:

  • $A_i \rightarrow 0$: One of the coefficents become zero reducing the fitting function to a trivial single expoenential
  • $E_0 \sim E_1$: Both the energy values come very close to each other making the exponential term similar causing collapse.
  • $E_i >> E_j$: One of the energy term blows up pushing the negative exponential towards zero.

To prevent the above cases appearing in our study we only consider those solutions that have a fitting error ($\frac{1}{N} \sum \left[ \log(Y_{\text{True}}) - \log(Y_{\text{Prediction}}) \right]^2$) lower than the closed form single exponential fitting case.

For some states in the above plot we do see that no valid double exponential fitting was found. We evaluated using both annealing type case and a linear learning rate regime and found that even if annealing is promising on paper it seldom helps in this use case. The overall error fluctuation in the ground state energy for different cut indices do seem to reduce drastically.

Neural Guessing and Levenberg-Marquardt Algorithm

We saw in the previous section how the GD based approach even when assited with techniques such as annealing and restarts fail to discover the solutions to our fitting problem. To better understand we look into the loss landscape for the different parameter values of a given state. Given that our parameter set that we want to estimate is of the form $\boldsymbol{\theta} = [A_0, E_0, A_1, E_1]$ and has four independent variables that primarily dominate the loss landscape we segregate the parameters into the sets of coefficients ($A_i$) and exponents ($E_i$). One thing to note is $A_i$s can be trivially calculated using the normal form equation of a linear regression if $E_i$s for a system is known. We exploit this property to calculate the minimum error that can be achieved by your fitting function for our data for any given set of exponents/energies. We plot this variation of error for different energy sets to discover the nature of the loss landscape. For this specific demonstration we chose B0_st as our state of choice.

The above figure shows the distribution of error (as colormap) for different values of $E_0$ and $E_1$. To zoom into the distribution we use $log$ transformation on the error axis. The colorbar represents the mapping of the colors into numeric error value for the given transformation (for the $log$ plot the colorbar maps the $log(Error)$). As can be seen from the above plots that the lose surface suffers predominantly from extremely narrow and steep valley and long plateau regions where gradients disapper.

The very nature of the loss landscape caused GD based optimisation steps to violently oscilate near the solution region and stall in the plateau regions. To solve this problem we introduce the Neural Guessing Based Levenberg-Marquardt Algorithm (LMA-NG). We have already discussed how LMA acts like Gauss Newton (GN) algorithm when provided with a strong guess value and the quadratic convergence rate of the GN algorithm. Here we try to train a neural network on the exisiting data such that it accurate models the solution region (parametric regions that are very close to the global minima) such that elements from the said region can act as strong guess value candidate for a Guess-Newton or LMA type optimisation algorithm.

The above diagram represent the schematics of the Neural Network component of the LMA-NG algorithm. The input to the network is an $N$-dimensional noise vector $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$ (where $N=64$), sampled from a standard multivariate normal distribution:

$$ \mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}_N) $$

This vector propagates through a sequence of hidden layers, each performing a composite operation consisting of an affine transformation followed by a non-linear activation. The Linear Layers perform an affine transformation defined as:

$$ \mathbf{h}^{(k)} = \mathbf{W}^{(k)}\mathbf{h}^{(k-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(k)} $$

where $\mathbf{W}^{(k)}$ and $\mathbf{b}^{(k)}$ are learnable parameters. The output of each linear layer is passed through a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function, defined element-wise as: $$\text{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$$ The model finally outputs the associated guess values as $A_i$s and $E_i$s after passing the input noise through all the non-linear transformations. The following losses are used to train the neural network:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{fit}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ \log(C(t_i)) - \log\left( A e^{-E_0 t_i} + B e^{-E_1 t_i} + \epsilon\right) \right]^2 $$ $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{div}} = \sum_{k=0}^{1} \left( \max(0, 1 - \sigma(E_k)) + \max(0, 1 - \sigma(A_k)) \right) $$

where $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability.

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{fit}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{div}} $$

The fitting loss, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{fit}}$ ensures that the neural network learns the region of parameter space where the fitting error is minimum for a double exponential fit. The diversity regularization term, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{div}}$, prevents mode collapse by enforcing a minimum variance in the predicted parameters across the batch. It is formulated using a hinge loss that penalizes the standard deviation $\sigma$ of the amplitudes $\mathbf{A}$ and energies $\mathbf{E}$ if they fall below a unit margin. This term encourages the network to explore the parameter space and maintain a diverse distribution of solutions.

cas_q_hl_32

cas_q_hl_32 is less noisy compared to the B_st case but it suffers from the problem of extremely high excited state contamination for the ground state energy value. This can be see particularly in the first (top-left) plot for the single exponential fitting case where no meaningful plateau region is found till the later part of the temporal region. Given how clean the sample is all the three techniques involving double-expontial fitting perform predictibly, but as expected the variation in the ground state-energy estimation does improve significanly as we move from simple LMA to the hybrid LMA-NG. We do see atleast a two orders of magnitude difference in the variation of the ground state energy between the baseline LMA based double exponential fitting and the LMA-NG algorithm.

cas_c_hh_12

cas_c_hh_12 is an example of a signal with a low excited energy contamination to the ground state. The single exponential fitting in this case manages to find a wide plateau region. The vanilla LMA algorithm for double exponential also manages to capture a wide plateau region followed by extreme noise later at the end. One interesting observation in this case is the failure of the gradient descent based approaches where a double exponential function could not be fitted properly and the fitting collapsed to single exponentials. The double exponetial fitting with LMA-NG does seem to perform well hinting towards the reliablity of the method. For this particular case too we see atleast a two orders of magnitude difference in the variation of the ground state energy between the baseline LMA based double exponential fitting and the LMA-NG algorithm.

lambcs_bc_32

lambcs_bc_32 is an extremely noisy sample but with low degree of excited state energy contamination. For the single exponential plot analysis we do see a plateau region. The double exponential fitting case with the vanilla LMA works nicely for the first few temporal states but diverges later on due to excessive noise. The gradient descent based fitting works fine for the entire spectrum but is completely overpowered by the neural guessing algorithm than has atleast a two order of magnitude smaller error completely overshadowing the gradient descent based algorithm.
